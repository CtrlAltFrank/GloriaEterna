# Backstory

Crooks was once an oathman of the great house, Mackenzie, steel in his hand, fire in his blood, death in his shadow. A tacksman. A vanguard. A man who led from the front and never looked back. When the horns sounded and men screamed, he was already in the thick of it, carving a path with his claymore. He believed in the cause: freedom, tradition, a new found sovereignty. He believed in his Lord. That was his first mistake. The house leader broke. He bent the knee to the new Avralian empire like a dog showing its belly. In return for mercy, he signed away the soul of his people. No sovereignty, no blades, no standing army, no future. A culture gutted, hung up like meat, and left to rot under imperial law. Crooks spat on his old oaths and walked into the wild with other oathbreakers like him, men hollowed out by betrayal, held together only by rage. At first, they struck back. Imperial caravans, outposts, watchmen’s barracks. They hit hard, fast, and dirty. The people called them rebels, heroes, wolves. But wolves grow hungry. Heroes lose purpose. And rebels turn into something worse. Over time, the raids lost meaning. They robbed the weak, bled the poor, and burned out homes with children inside. Gold jingled louder than conscience. Crooks watched his men become feral. Thieves. Murderers. No different from the empire they once swore to fight. Still, they listened to him, barely.

One grey morning, they came across a shepherd and his flock. Just a quiet man with a crook and a loyal mutt. Crooks, sick of the blood and smoke, said, “He’s got nothing. Let’s move on.” The men nodded. Then one of them cracked the shepherd’s skull open with a club. Just like that. A twitch of the arm, and the man was dead. His dog wailed, circling the corpse, nudging it with its nose. It didn’t understand that he was gone. One of the gang growled, “Shut that mutt up,” and stepped forward, cudgel raised. Crooks broke. He screamed, guttural and broken like something tearing loose inside him, and charged. His claymore came down like judgment, splitting the man’s head clean down the center. Bone. Blood. Silence. He yanked the blade free and turned to the others. His eyes told them everything. Come forward, and I’ll kill you all. And they believed him. These weren’t men anymore. They were jackals fat on carrion. And Crooks was a dying lion who still had one roar left. They backed off, shame-faced, and disappeared into the hills like smoke.

Crooks stood alone, the claymore dripping in his hands, the dog at his feet whining over its master’s corpse. He looked at the blade. He hated what it had made of him, what he had let himself become. He dropped it in the dirt. Instead, he picked up the shepherd’s crook, simple wood, worn smooth from years of honest work, and began gathering the scattered sheep. That day, Crooks ceased being a warrior, a rebel, or even a man of honor. He became a shepherd. A guardian of silence and innocence in a world that had long since forgotten either.

For years, Crooks lived quiet. He tended the sheep, mended fences, and let the world forget his name. The dog grew old, slow, half-blind, but not alone. She had a pup, lean and sharp-eyed, always underfoot, always watching, though eventually the mother hound passed. Crooks never named it. He just called, and it followed. Seasons passed. He thought perhaps peace could be something earned, even for men like him. But peace is a lie the empire tells the broken so they stay still. 

One spring morning, a young well-off imperial soldier rode up the hill to his pasture, armor gleaming, voice high and rehearsed. “By imperial decree,” the boy said, “this flock is now requisitioned for military supply.” Crooks stared at him. The sheep grazed, unbothered. The young dog lifted her head. Crooks said nothing for a long while, then finally spoke, his voice low and cold as old stone. “Turn around. There is nothing here worth dying over.” The boy puffed up. “You will comply!”

Crooks didn’t move. “Last warning, lad. These sheep aren’t yours. Not today.” The soldier reached for the reins to retrieve more men. Crooks stepped forward, pulled him from the saddle with his great hook, and delivered him into the earth. The boy shouted, struggled, squirmed, grasped out breathlessly for mercy he hadn’t earned. Crooks didn’t answer. He slammed the blunt end of the shepherd’s crook through his skull, wood through flesh, bone through brain, a squelch, then stillness. No hesitation. No cruelty. Just the old rhythm of violence, practiced and precise. Greymatter coated the grass. The pup whimpered, then went quiet.

They came for him three days later. Shackles. Beatings. No trial. No defense. The empire didn’t care who he was or what he had once done. All they saw was a strong back and a dangerous past. They threw him into the meat grinder, the penal legions, where old killers are repurposed as fresh meat for distant wars. Branded with iron, stripped of name, given only a number and an order. Crooks marched again, not for a house, not for honor, but for the same empire that shattered both.

# House Mackenzie
House Mackenzie of Gavaria was once among the proudest bloodlines in the island city’s high council. Warriors, statesmen, and defenders of tradition, they held fast against the wildness beyond the stone walls. First among them was Lord Murtagh Mackenzie, a commander both feared and beloved, whose blade had seen more wars than the city had towers. Crooks had followed him since youth, not by birthright but by choice, drawn to the fire in Murtagh’s spirit and the iron in his judgment. They led together in campaign after campaign, pushing back the creeping chaos of Fae Barbaria, where dusk never fell and the forest itself hungered for flesh.

In those brutal years, Murtagh suffered a wound that never truly healed. Though the blade barely nicked him, something darker clung to the cut. His skin began to blacken at the veins, his breath came in short rasps, and his sleep was plagued by visions. The healers called it The Festering, an old curse known to cling to wounds dealt by cursed fae weapons. Though he made a full recovery in the flesh, those close to him saw the change. The fatigue, the doubt, the tremble behind his voice. Still, he ruled, proud and unbending.

Anticipating the end, Murtagh named his younger brother Dougal as regent in the event of his death, trusting blood more than the shifting loyalties of council politics. Not long after this decree, Murtagh collapsed in his chamber, clawing at his throat with foam wetting his beard. The court whispered apoplexy, though others, in secret, spoke of poison or lingering hexes. His body was burned quickly. No inquest was held.

Weeks later, as mourning turned to unrest, war came. House Fontana of Avralius, bolstered by imperial coin, marched on Gavaria. The siege was swift. The walls cracked and the gates buckled. The defenders fought hard but not long. Blood ran in the streets. Crooks and the other oathmen prepared to make their final stand, but the order never came. In the high chamber, the council convened to decide the city's fate. There, Dougal cast the vote that broke the line.

He chose to surrender.

He claimed it was to preserve lives and spare the city from ruin. The vote passed by a narrow margin. Those who opposed the decision, loyalists and warriors who believed in resistance, were executed, exiled, or fell in the last clashes. The great houses were disarmed. The armies were folded into the imperial legions or dissolved outright. Soldiers deserted in droves. Oathmen like Crooks fled to the wilds, betrayed and cast adrift. Others turned mercenary, outlaw, or worse.

In exchange for his loyalty, Dougal was named Lord Mackenzie, though few spoke the title without a bitter taste. The true heir, Colum, Murtagh’s son, was stripped of title and name. Declared a non-citizen, he was exiled from Gavaria. None have heard from him since.

Some speculate that Murtagh’s death was orchestrated by Avralius and Dougal as a way for Dougal to gain Lordship while having Gavaria surrender without a fight. Maybe if all of the houses took a stand, fate could have been altered…

So fell House Mackenzie. Not in fire or thunder, but in silence and ink. Not with a blade, but with a vote.
